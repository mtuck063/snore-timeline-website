Sleep Score & Weekly Insights

What your score means, what moves it, and how to read your week.

One night tells you what happened; a score and a weekly view tell you whether it matters. Snore Timeline condenses each night into a 0 to 100 sleep score, then rolls your nights into a Weekly Summary that compares this week to last. This page explains how the score is built, what each supporting metric measures, and how to use sleep factors to test what changes your nights. Everything here comes from recorded sound and any synced wearable data; none of it is a medical assessment.

The sleep score and its bands

The sleep score sits on a 0 to 100 scale and reflects your overall sleep quality for the night. Scores fall into four bands: Excellent (90 to 100), Good (75 to 89), Fair (60 to 74), and Poor (below 60).

Duration carries the most weight. The app treats 7 or more hours of sleep as ideal. The penalty for falling short grows gently at first, because sleep need varies and a 6 to 7 hour night is fine for many adults, then steepens as the night gets shorter. Beyond duration, the score weighs:

  • Deep and REM percentages. The app targets roughly 13 to 23 percent Deep Sleep, with 20 percent or more earning credit, and about 20 to 25 percent REM. Healthy amounts in these ranges can add points. Because estimating Deep and REM from audio is hard, the app goes easy on low readings and credits quiet, restful Silence toward your restorative sleep. Sleep Stages explains why.
  • Time to fall asleep. Taking longer than 30 minutes lowers the score.
  • Continuity. How well you stayed asleep once you fell asleep, built from your time awake after sleep onset (WASO) and your number of awakenings. The two combine into one capped penalty, so a fragmented night is charged once rather than once per metric. Sleep efficiency still appears as its own metric in your summaries, but inside the score, continuity took its place.
  • Bedtime regularity. A bedtime within an hour of your usual one is neutral; drifting further costs points.
  • Snoring and breathing disruptions. Snoring counts against the score based on how often it happens per hour. Breathing disruptions can lower it noticeably, and more disruptions cause larger reductions.

Tap the score badge to see a detailed breakdown of how each factor affected your night. Treat the score as a guide to your sleep habits, never as a clinical diagnosis.

Synced wearables can change the score too. When the app syncs sleep data from Apple Health, any nights that also have a recording are re-analyzed so the score can use the Deep and REM stages from your wearable, which beat audio estimates for accuracy. That means a night's score may refine itself once your Apple Watch finishes finalizing its staging and the data reaches the app. A wearable's recovery score also feeds back into the sleep score: an unusually strong or weak night of vitals nudges it up or down by as much as 15 points around a neutral baseline, while a typical night changes nothing. Apple Watch & Wearables covers the sync.

The recovery score

The sleep score rates how you slept. The recovery score answers a different question: how well did your body recover overnight? Where the sleep score reads your recording, the recovery score reads your overnight vitals, so it needs a wearable that writes to Apple Health, such as an Apple Watch, Oura Ring, Whoop, Garmin, or Ultrahuman. Without a connected wearable, the app shows your sleep score on its own and leaves recovery out.

Six inputs feed the score, and each is measured against your own rolling baseline rather than a fixed target, so "good" means good for you:

  • Heart rate variability (HRV). Higher variability points to a recovered nervous system. A naturally low HRV is never penalized below its own baseline.
  • Resting heart rate. The steady low your heart settled to overnight. A genuinely low rate, the kind fitness builds, lifts the score on its own.
  • Respiratory rate. Steady, typical breathing scores well; a clear rise above your norm pulls it down.
  • Blood oxygen (SpO2). Read against a healthy floor around 95 percent.
  • Wrist temperature. Deviation from your baseline, on watches that record it.
  • Time asleep. Scored against a roughly 7-hour need rather than your baseline, because sleep need is fairly absolute.

The result is a 0 to 100 score shown as a five-segment charge bar with a one-word verdict:

  • Excellent: 82 or above, a strong night across the board
  • Good: 74 to 81, clearly above your norm
  • Fair: 58 to 73, at or near your baseline, the everyday default
  • Low: 45 to 57, a real signal below your norm
  • Poor: below 45

Holding your baseline lands in Fair on purpose: recovery is "maintaining," not a win. Good and Excellent are earned by beating your norm. A short night caps the score no matter how good the vitals look, because you cannot fully recover on too little sleep: under 5 hours holds it to Low at most, and under 4 hours to Poor. Tap the score to see the per-metric breakdown, where each vital shows its value, its direction against your baseline, and a plain-language note on what drove the night.

Because the recovery score already accounts for a short night, the sleep score's own duration penalty is kept moderate, so a naturally short night is not charged twice across the two scores.

In the morning summary, the two scores combine into a single status line that pairs your time asleep with a recovery verdict, for example "6h 42m · Well Recovered" or "Short but Recovered." The wording reflects both how long and how well you recovered. Like every metric here, the recovery score is for personal insight and is never a clinical readiness measure or a diagnosis.

Your morning summary

After each recording, the app builds a nightly summary so you can take in the whole night at a glance. It shows your sleep score with its band, a breakdown of time spent in Light, Deep, REM, and Awake stages, your average respiratory rate in breaths per minute, and your snoring and breathing disruption activity. Tapping the score badge expands the factor-by-factor breakdown showing what helped and what hurt.

The summary works as an at-a-glance read on your habits and trends, while the timeline remains the place to dig into individual sounds.

The Snoring card inside Sleep Insights goes further than a count. It compares your snoring rate in the minutes before each awakening against the night's average, and says what it finds in plain words, for example that snoring climbed 66 percent just before your wakes, a pattern suggesting the snoring itself may be disturbing your sleep. An awakening browser then pages through every wake of the night on a snores-per-minute chart, magnifies the five minutes before and after each one, and writes the story out: how far into the night it came, how hard you were snoring beforehand against your usual rate, and how things settled afterward.

Sleep Efficiency

Sleep Efficiency answers a precise question: of the time you spent in bed, how much did you spend asleep? The app divides time asleep by total time in bed and reports the percentage. High efficiency means you fell asleep fast and stayed asleep with little time awake in bed; low efficiency means the recording contains a lot of wakefulness.

Snore Timeline rates the percentage on five levels:

  • Excellent: 90% or above
  • Optimal: 85 to 90%
  • Good: 80 to 85%
  • Fair: 75 to 80%
  • Poor: below 75%

The Weekly Summary shows efficiency as a weekly average, which smooths out the odd restless night and gives you a steadier signal than any single recording.

WASO: time awake after falling asleep

WASO stands for Wake After Sleep Onset: the total time you spent awake after first falling asleep. Efficiency tells you how much of the night you slept; WASO tells you how broken that sleep was once it started. Two nights with identical efficiency can feel different if one packed its wakefulness into a single stretch and the other scattered it across the night.

The app rates WASO in five tiers:

  • Minimal disruption: under 15 minutes
  • Normal: 15 to 30 minutes
  • Moderate disruption: 30 to 45 minutes
  • High disruption: 45 to 60 minutes
  • Severe fragmentation: 60 minutes or more

Lower WASO points to more continuous sleep. Like every metric on this page, it serves personal insight, not clinical measurement.

How Snore Timeline measures WASO

The WASO clock starts the moment the app first detects sleep. Wakefulness before that belongs to your time to fall asleep, so a long wind-down in bed cannot inflate your WASO. From then on, every awake period adds its duration to the total.

The app reads wakefulness from movement sounds, room noise, and breathing patterns inconsistent with sleep, and picking up your phone during the night registers as an awakening directly. A suspected awakening only counts after roughly 90 seconds with no sign of sleep, so the brief stirs everyone has between sleep cycles pass without registering. On nights where your sleep data comes from a synced wearable, WASO uses the wearable's awake staging instead of the audio estimate.

What counts as a normal WASO

Waking at night is part of normal sleep architecture. Adults surface briefly at the end of each sleep cycle, several times a night, and forget nearly all of it by morning. A WASO of 15 to 30 minutes sits in the app's Normal band, and under 15 minutes reads as minimal disruption. Sleep also grows lighter with age, so an older sleeper's typical WASO runs higher than a younger one's.

A single rough night says little. Stress, a late meal, or an unfamiliar room can push one night into a higher band. The pattern across a week gives you the steadier signal, the same way it does for efficiency.

WASO, awakenings, and efficiency

The app reports three views of wakefulness, and each answers its own question. Awakenings count how many times you woke. WASO totals how long you stayed awake. Sleep efficiency divides time asleep by time in bed, which folds your time to fall asleep into the picture. Ten one-minute wakes and one ten-minute wake produce the same WASO with very different nights behind them, so the score breakdown checks your average wake length and words its verdict around what actually happened: many brief wakes you settled back from, or a few long ones.

How WASO moves your sleep score

WASO feeds the continuity factor together with your awakening count. Up to 20 minutes awake costs nothing. Beyond that, the penalty grows in steps past 30, 45, and 60 minutes, waking more than a couple of times adds a little more, and the combined total is capped, so even a badly fragmented night cannot crater the score through continuity alone. Tap the score badge and the Continuity row shows the exact points next to your night's numbers, for example "3 awakenings · 42m awake".

Lowering a high WASO

The recording tells you more than the number does. Open the timeline around an awake period and listen to what came right before it: a loud snore, a noise in the room, a partner, a pet. Waking that follows snoring or breathing disruptions points somewhere different than waking after a barking dog.

Then test your suspects with sleep factors. Alcohol tends to fragment the second half of the night, and Late Meal, Caffeine, Stress, Hot Room, and Nap are common culprits worth tagging. After enough tagged and untagged nights, the comparison shows whether your WASO actually differs in your own data, and the Test a Remedy guide walks through the workflow. A steady bedtime helps too; the Consistency Score tracks it.

If a high WASO keeps company with frequent breathing disruptions, that combination is worth showing to a doctor. Export & Sharing turns your nights into a report you can bring along, and the CSV export carries a WASO column for every night.

Bedtime Consistency Score

Going to bed at a regular hour matters enough that the app scores it on its own. The Consistency Score reflects how regular your bedtime has been over the last 14 days, based on how much it varies from night to night. A 1-hour buffer applies: variation up to about an hour still scores 100, and the score drops as your bedtimes spread out further.

The app takes each night's bedtime from your earliest recording start time, so starting the recording when you get into bed keeps this metric honest. The Weekly Summary also shows your average Bedtime across the week.

Tip

If you use a Recording Delay to skip your wind-down time, start the session at the same point in your routine each night. Consistency measures the recording start, and a steady ritual keeps the measurement meaningful.

Sleep Deficit

Sleep Deficit appears in the Weekly Summary and shows the gap between how much you slept and a 7-hour nightly target, totaled across the whole week. A positive number means you fell short of the target over the week; a negative number means you slept more than the target, a sleep surplus.

It works alongside Time Asleep, which shows your average nightly duration for the week. The two answer different questions: Time Asleep tells you what a typical night looked like, while Sleep Deficit tells you what the whole week added up to. A few short weeknights can hide inside a decent average yet still leave a deficit you can see at a glance.

Sleep factors and correlations

Sleep factors turn your recordings into experiments. You tag what you did, the app compares tagged nights against untagged ones, and patterns emerge from your own data instead of guesswork.

What you can track

The app includes 23 built-in factors across four categories:

  • Substances: Alcohol, Caffeine, Medication, Smoking
  • Lifestyle: Exercise, Late Meal, Stress, Late Screen Time, Nap, Fatigue
  • Environment: Slept on Back, Slept on Side, Elevated Pillow, Hot Room, Cold Room
  • Health: Nasal Strips, Congestion, Allergies, Cold/Flu, CPAP, Mouth Guard, Oral Appliance, Mouth Taping

If the list misses something, create a custom factor by giving it a name and choosing one of those four categories. You tag factors on your nightly summaries, and tagging selectively is the whole trick: only tag a factor on nights you had it. Tag something every single night and there are no factor-free nights left to compare against, so no correlation can appear.

Building Insights and unlocking comparisons

Correlations need data before they mean anything. To unlock the comparison for a factor you need at least 5 total tracked nights, with at least 2 nights tagged and at least 2 nights without the tag. Nights with synced wearable sleep data count too, even without an audio recording. Until you cross those thresholds, a Building Insights state shows a progress bar toward unlocking, how many more nights you still need, and the factors you have tracked so far. The app looks back over your last 8 weeks of data to build the patterns, and more nights make the results more confident.

Reading the results

Once unlocked, the "What Affected Your Sleep" card in the Weekly Summary ranks your factors as plain-language statements, strongest first, for example "Elevated Pillow cut breathing disruptions 72%". Each row carries a verdict drawn from an impact score: Better sleep means the factor is associated with improvement in your data, Worse sleep with a decline, Mixed signals means some metrics moved in your favor while others declined, and No clear effect means the difference between your with and without nights was too small to call.

Tap a row to expand it. The expanded view shows how much each metric changed on tagged nights versus untagged ones, with a change bar per metric: snore time, events per hour, breathing disruptions, sleep talking, sleep score, and sleep efficiency, plus resting heart rate, HRV, and breathing rate when a wearable feeds Apple Health. Those wrist vitals also feed the impact score, so a factor that mostly moves your physiology, like stress, can still rank as your strongest. Below the bars sit a short line on the likely mechanism, a coaching tip, and a footer with the night counts for each group and the reminder "Association, not cause." Factors that have not crossed the thresholds yet show how many more tagged nights they need instead of expanding.

A concrete example: CPAP is a built-in factor. Tag it on the nights you used your machine and leave other nights untagged. After 5 total nights with at least 2 in each group, the CPAP row shows whether your machine nights carried less snore time, fewer disruptions, and a higher score. The Breathing Disruptions page and the Test a Remedy guide walk through this workflow in full.

Correlations show patterns in your own recordings and may reflect other habits happening at the same time. Treat them as personal insight to discuss with your doctor, never as cause-and-effect or a diagnosis.

Weekly trends

The Weekly Summary gives you a 7-day overview of your snoring and sleep data and compares it against the previous week. Each metric shows a week-over-week change, as a percentage for most metrics; Peak Volume, measured in decibels, shows the difference in dB instead. When the previous week had no data, the change reads 0% in a neutral color rather than flagging a trend that does not exist. Daily charts plot each metric across the last 7 days so you can see the shape of the week, not just its total.

The summary averages a wide set of metrics:

  • Snoring: Snore Time, Snoring Signals, Signals Per Hour, plus breathing disruptions and sleep talking (Sleep Talking Time and Talking Signals).
  • Sleep quality: Sleep Score, Time Asleep, Sleep Efficiency, Sleep Deficit, Deep Sleep (ideal 13 to 23%), REM Sleep (ideal 20 to 25%), Awakenings, Bedtime, and the Consistency Score.
  • Highlights: the Top 5 Notable Episodes from the week, which link back to episode details.

Your average Sleep Score for the week appears with its own week-over-week change and a 7-day chart, so you can watch the score move night by night instead of judging a single morning.

To answer the question most people open this screen for, "is my snoring getting better or worse", watch three numbers: Snore Time (total time spent snoring), Snoring Signals (the total count of snore-related events including snoring, coughs, gasps, and disruptions), and Signals Per Hour (signals detected per hour of recording). A downward trend in these from one week to the next means less snoring activity overall, and the daily charts show which nights drove the change.

These are personal-insight metrics for tracking patterns over time. For sharing them with a doctor, Export & Sharing shows how to turn a week of data into a report.
