Breathing Disruption Detection

What the app listens for, how accurate it is, and how to read the numbers.

Snore Timeline listens for moments when your airway closes and air stops getting through. A detection model, trained on sleep-lab recordings and run on your phone, marks each one as a breathing disruption. This page covers what the model listens for, how accurate it is, how to read the card and the timeline, and where listening alone runs out.

What is a breathing disruption?

A breathing disruption is a stretch, usually 10 to 20 seconds, where your airway closes and little or no air reaches your lungs. Your chest keeps trying to breathe. Then the airway opens and breathing comes back, often with a snort, a gasp or a deep breath.

Most of the disruptions the app finds are silent: about 70% of them. Snoring stops while the airway is closed, then starts again. In the rest, snoring or choking carries on while you strain against the blocked airway. Each disruption in the list says which kind it was.

How the app finds them

The model learned from hundreds of hours of sleep-lab recordings. In those nights, sensors at the nose showed exactly when breathing was blocked, so the model could learn what those moments sound like to a microphone.

  1. It listens to your breathing sounds. Four times a second it reads the pitch of the sound, how regular it is, and how loud it is. It doesn't need total silence, so it can catch disruptions where you keep snoring or choke.
  2. It hears the minutes around each moment. It judges every second with several minutes of audio on either side: the snoring before, the pause or strain, and the snort or gasp when breathing restarts.
  3. It marks stretches of 8 seconds or more that sound like a blocked airway. Two marks a few seconds apart count as one.
  4. It only counts the time you're asleep, using the app's own sleep stages, and only stretches with snoring within 5 minutes either side. Quiet stretches far from any snoring are left alone.

Because the model works in 5-minute blocks, a mark shows up 2 to 7 minutes after it happens, and the first result needs about 11 minutes of recording. The card updates during the night, so you can check it before you stop. Everything runs on your phone. No audio leaves it.

How accurate it is

These numbers come from sleep-lab testing on people who snore or breathe loud enough for the phone to hear, which is who Snore Timeline is built for.

  • When it marks a disruption, it's right 99% of the time. Sensors at the nose showed the airway was fully or partly blocked at that moment.
  • It finds about 60% of full blockages, but few partial ones, where the airway narrows without closing.
  • Treat your number as a minimum. A sleep study counts full and partial blockages, so in testing it found about 3 times as many as the app. For people whose airway mostly closes fully it was closer to 1.5 times. In testing the app never counted more than the sleep study did.

If you breathe quietly, or your airway mostly narrows rather than closes, the app hears less and misses more. Your trend across many nights tells you more than any single night.

Reading your number

The main number is disruptions per hour of sleep. The card rates it in one of four bands:

Once you have 5 or more nights from the last two weeks, the card also compares tonight with your usual night: more, fewer, or about the same. The card needs at least 30 minutes of sleep.

What the card shows

The breathing disruption card sits under the Snore Score in your nightly summary. Tap the Snore Score header to open it. Below the rate and band you'll find:

  • Clusters. Disruptions that came within 2 minutes of each other, with the total and the longest cluster.
  • Time disrupted. The share of your sleep spent in a disruption.
  • Typical length and the longest one, plus a breakdown of how long they lasted: 8 to 15, 15 to 25, 25 to 40, and 40 seconds or more.
  • Through the night. A bar for every 15 minutes, with REM sleep shaded if you wore an Apple Watch. Tap a bar to see only those disruptions and play them.
  • The list. Each disruption reads like "14 silent seconds, then a gasp" or "11 seconds of choked snoring". The strongest ones carry the label Likely a full airway blockage: silent for 15 seconds or more, or ended with a loud gasp. Tap play to hear it or jump to that moment on the timeline.
  • REM sleep. With 20 minutes or more of Apple Watch REM, the card shows whether your disruptions bunched up in dreaming sleep or spread across the night.
  • Blood oxygen. Your lowest and average reading, if your Apple Watch recorded it.

The "How are breathing disruptions detected?" button under the card opens the full explanation in the app.

On your timeline

Each disruption shows on the waveform as a faint pale-purple band at every zoom level, so you can scan a whole night and spot clusters. On the snoring chart, tap a disruption to see whether the airway was likely fully blocked, how long airflow stopped, what sound breathing restarted with, and your nearest blood oxygen and sleep stage. Playing it back lands differently than reading a number, and the audio is what makes it worth bringing to a doctor.

Nights from before the update

The earlier detector only counted a long silence followed by a gasp, so it found far fewer disruptions. Nights recorded before the update keep their old counts and old card, marked "Recorded with the earlier detector". Their audio is gone, so they can't be checked again. Expect your numbers to jump on new nights. That's the old detector undercounting, not your breathing getting worse. Compare recent nights with each other, not with older ones. The Android app still uses the earlier detector.

Gasps vs. breathing disruptions

A gasp is one sound, a sudden forceful breath in, and the app still logs it as its own event on the timeline. A breathing disruption is the whole blocked stretch. When a disruption ends with a gasp, cough, loud snort or loud breath, the list names it, so you can see which gasps followed a blockage. Episodes & Events covers gasps with the other event types.

Disruptions also feed into your sleep score and show in your Weekly Summary and factor comparisons.

CPAP nights and quiet breathers

Detection works by listening, so it depends on hearing your breathing. The model also skips stretches with no snoring nearby. Quiet breathing, a CPAP mask that keeps you from snoring, or a phone that's too far away can all mean fewer marks. Listening alone misses:

  • Partial blockages, where the airway narrows without closing
  • Disruptions in quiet breathers, where there's little sound to hear
  • Anything buried under a fan, air purifier or white noise
  • How far your oxygen dropped after each one, and which position you slept in

The model was trained and tested on PSG-Audio sleep-lab recordings, and the app has not been clinically validated.

Tip

Put your phone on the nightstand near your head, not under a pillow or blanket, and turn off loud fans and white noise if you can. Wear your Apple Watch to add sleep stages and blood oxygen. The placement guide covers the setup.

Medical disclaimer

Medical disclaimer

This is audio analysis only. Snore Timeline is not a medical device. It can't diagnose sleep apnea or any other condition. It measures sound patterns. It doesn't measure blood oxygen, airflow, chest movement, or brain activity, which are what a professional sleep assessment relies on. If you're seeing frequent breathing disruptions, or you're sleepy during the day, wake up with headaches, or a partner has noticed pauses in your breathing, take the results to a healthcare professional.

The results make a good conversation starter. The Doctor-Ready Data guide shows how to turn your recordings into something a doctor can use, and Export & Sharing covers the report and audio files you can bring to an appointment. Everything stays on your device until you decide to share it. See the Privacy Policy.
