Snore Timeline vs SnoreLab: Privacy, Price and Recording

Price, App Store privacy labels, audio playback, and breathing features, checked on both apps’ official pages on 27 September 2026.

By Menelik Tucker · Updated 28 Sep 2026

Quick answer

Snore Timeline has no subscription and no in-app purchases, and its App Store privacy label reads Data Not Collected. SnoreLab has a limited free tier, sells Premium for $5.99 to $59.99 on the App Store, and declares data in all three label tiers, including Usage Data used to track you. My app records the whole night and puts every sound, sleep talking included, on a zoomable timeline, then gives the night a 0 to 100 sleep score with snoring as one factor. It also estimates your sleep stages from the recording itself, so you don’t need a wearable or Apple Health, while SnoreLab’s sleep phases come from an Apple Watch through Apple Health. SnoreLab’s answer is the Snore Score. It’s a single snoring intensity number, and Premium adds breathing metrics.

Key takeaways

  • On price, Snore Timeline charges nothing and sells nothing, while SnoreLab’s free version can’t run on 2 consecutive days once you’re past your first 5 sessions, per its FAQ.
  • The privacy labels differ. As declared on the App Store on 27 September 2026, Snore Timeline’s says Data Not Collected, and SnoreLab’s lists data used to track you, data linked to you and data not linked to you.
  • For audio, SnoreLab saves 20 audio samples per night by default and keeps the full night only on Premium, while Snore Timeline records and plays back the whole night for everyone.
  • SnoreLab’s strengths are 57,000 ratings at 4.7, the Snore Score, and more than 170 million monitored nights, per its site. Both apps let you tag remedies and create your own, and Snore Timeline scores the whole night from 0 to 100 and compares tagged and untagged nights across 23 built-in sleep factors.
  • On breathing, both work from audio. They do not diagnose sleep apnea. SnoreLab reports BreathFlow and a Breathing Disturbance Index (Premium), while Snore Timeline flags 10-second silences followed by a recovery sound.
  • Both run on iPhone and Android, so platform won’t decide it. Snore Timeline includes an Apple Watch app and reads Apple Health data from Apple Watch, Oura, Whoop, Garmin and Ultrahuman, and SnoreLab reads Apple Watch sleep phases through Apple Health.

    Price

    Snore Timeline is free. No subscription, no in-app purchases, no premium tier. What you download on night one is what you keep.

    SnoreLab is a free download with a real free tier. According to its FAQ, the free version lets you record your night, check charts and stats in the morning, listen to captured audio, and tag remedies and factors. Things change after your first 5 sessions. From then on, the free version can’t run on 2 consecutive days.

    Premium removes that limit and, per the FAQ, adds a long list of extras: BreathFlow and the Breathing Disturbance Index, full session history, full night recording, trends, audio backup, export, a rest rating, and Soundscapes.

    On the App Store there are ten Premium purchase options, from $5.99 to $59.99, covering monthly, three-month and yearly plans plus a one-time purchase at $7.99. Which price a new user sees, the listing doesn’t say.

    The free tier is fine for a week of testing. Record every night and you’ll need Premium. Snoring apps with no subscription takes a wider look at paywalls.

    Privacy

    Every App Store listing has a privacy label. The developer fills it in, and Apple sorts the answers into three tiers: data used to track you, data linked to you, and data not linked to you.

    On 27 September 2026, the two apps declared the following.

    • Snore Timeline declares Data Not Collected. I didn’t build an account system, a server or an analytics SDK into it, so recordings stay on your phone.
    • SnoreLab declares data in all three tiers, and the table below lists each item with the purpose SnoreLab gives for it.

    Audio Data on a label means the developer declared that it collects audio. On its own, it doesn’t tell you that every night uploads. SnoreLab’s FAQ fills in the rest: recordings are saved on your device and “are not uploaded to a server without your permission.” Turn on Audio Backup, a Premium feature, and SnoreLab stores your audio on its server, encrypted with a key unique to you. Snore detection runs on the device with no internet connection, the FAQ says, though uploading sessions to a SnoreLab account needs one.

    What each app records

    Snore Timeline

    Snore Timeline records the whole night through the phone microphone, or through AirPods and other Bluetooth mics, and analyzes every sound as it happens with no periodic sampling. Snores, gasps, coughs, sleep talking and breathing each get a label. For sounds the classifier can’t name, such as whispers, there’s a Loud Sound Detection option. How detection works covers each label. You can tag any night with remedies and other factors, from 23 built-in ones such as Mouth Guard, Nasal Strips or Slept on Side to ones you create, and after 5 tracked nights, at least 2 tagged and 2 untagged, the app compares them and ranks what helped. The test a remedy guide walks through it.

    SnoreLab

    SnoreLab listens all night and detects snoring. By default it saves 20 audio samples per night, its site says, and Full Night Recording, which keeps every captured sound, is a Premium feature. You can add notes and tag remedies and factors, and when the preset list falls short you can create your own. SnoreLab says it has monitored more than 170 million nights of sleep. That’s a big base for its Snore Score benchmarks.

    Neither SnoreLab’s site nor its App Store listing names sleep talking as a detected sound. If you want to catch what you say at night, my sleep talking recorder guide has more on that.

    Two scores, two timelines

    Both apps sum up a night in one number and draw it as a chart. The numbers don’t measure the same thing.

    Snore Timeline’s sleep score

    Snore Timeline gives each night a sleep score from 0 to 100. Duration carries the most weight, and 7 hours or more counts as ideal. After that it weighs your Deep and REM share, time to fall asleep, continuity (time awake after falling asleep plus the number of awakenings) and bedtime regularity. Snoring and breathing disruptions pull it down. Snoring costs points by how often it happens per hour, while breathing disruptions cost more, with a bigger hit the more of them there are.

    There are four bands, Excellent (90 to 100), Good (75 to 89), Fair (60 to 74) and Poor (below 60), and every score lands in one. Tap the score badge to see, factor by factor, what helped and what hurt. Sleep score & weekly insights goes through each factor.

    If you have a wearable that writes to Apple Health, you also get a separate recovery score, which reads HRV, resting heart rate, respiratory rate, blood oxygen and wrist temperature against your own baseline, plus time asleep. It can move the sleep score up or down by as much as 15 points. The Weekly Summary sets this week against last for Sleep Score, Snore Time, Signals Per Hour, efficiency, sleep deficit and more.

    SnoreLab’s Snore Score

    SnoreLab’s FAQ describes the Snore Score as its own measure of snoring intensity, worked out from the average volume and total duration of suspected snoring episodes in a night. Higher means louder or more frequent snoring. It has no units. According to SnoreLab, someone who snores loud for a short stretch can score close to someone who snores quiet all night.

    SnoreLab sorts snoring into Light, Loud and Epic, and as a rough guide it treats loud as 50 to 60 dB and epic as 60 dB and up. A typical new user scores around 25. Hit 100 or more and you’re in the highest 15% of SnoreLab users. The score is there to compare nights and test remedies. That pairing is SnoreLab’s core strength.

    Snore Timeline’s timeline and episodes

    Snore Timeline draws the night as a waveform you can drag and zoom, from 1-second bars all the way out to 1-hour ticks. Each sound type has its color (dusty rose for snoring, cream for sleep talking, pale purple for breathing disruptions, gray for silence). Tap anywhere to hear that moment. The colors and zoom levels are explained in Timeline & playback. On the nightly summary, the snoring chart switches between loudness and snores per minute, and a band of your sleep stages runs underneath it, so you can see whether your snoring clusters in light, deep or REM sleep. Tap a moment on the hypnogram and the snoring chart marks the same time.

    Snores close together form an episode. A 30-second silence closes it, and the next snore starts a new one. For each episode you see its duration, snore count, peak and average dB, a rhythm class and a plain-English summary. Episodes & events has the rules.

    Testing a remedy works through tags. Pick any of 23 built-in sleep factors, such as CPAP, Mouth Guard, Nasal Strips or Slept on Side, or make a custom one. Once you have 5 tracked nights, with at least 2 tagged and 2 untagged, the app compares tagged nights against untagged ones and ranks what helped. The test a remedy guide goes step by step.

    What differs

    SnoreLab’s Snore Score measures snoring intensity. Snore Timeline’s sleep score rates the whole night, with snoring as one factor, and snoring on its own shows up in Snore Time and Signals Per Hour. Sleep stages work differently too. Snore Timeline estimates them from the audio on your phone, with no watch on your wrist, while SnoreLab’s FAQ describes its sleep phases coming from an Apple Watch through Apple Health.

    Breathing pauses

    Both apps listen to breathing through a microphone. They do not diagnose sleep apnea, and both say so.

    SnoreLab has two Premium metrics. BreathFlow is its own measure of how stable your breathing is during sleep, based on acoustic patterns, and a night above 90% reads as Stable. The Breathing Disturbance Index estimates breathing disturbances per hour, with anything above 12 events per hour marked Elevated. According to SnoreLab, BreathFlow was trained on close to 400 nights of sleep lab data and checked against Apple Watch data from thousands of home nights. Its site says both BreathFlow and the BDI are not medical devices and are not intended to diagnose, treat or prevent obstructive sleep apnea.

    Snore Timeline uses a fixed rule you can read: it waits for at least 1 minute of audible breathing, then looks for 10 or more seconds of silence ended by a recovery sound such as a loud snore, gasp or breath. Each match goes on the timeline for playback. It hasn’t been clinically validated, and it’s not a medical device. Breathing disruptions explains the rule and its limits.

    NIH’s MedlinePlus notes that snoring can be a sign of sleep apnea, and it advises seeing a provider if you wake up gasping or feel tired during the day. Whatever either app shows you, bring it to that appointment. My guide to apps that flag breathing pauses covers what audio can and can’t catch.

    Watch, Android, export

    Apple Watch

    An Apple Watch app comes bundled with Snore Timeline on iPhone. From your wrist you can stop a recording, get a Snore Nudge tap when you snore, and see heart rate, HRV, respiratory rate, blood oxygen and wrist temperature next to your snoring. Recordings start on the iPhone, though. Sleep and biometric data from Apple Watch, Oura, Whoop, Garmin and Ultrahuman comes in through Apple Health, feeds the sleep score and recovery score, and adds the wearable’s stages to the hypnogram. None of it is required for sleep stages, which Snore Timeline estimates from the recording. Details are in Apple Watch & biometrics.

    On an iPhone paired with an Apple Watch, SnoreLab’s FAQ says you can sync it with Apple Health to show sleep phases in the session chart. There’s no Watch app. The App Store listing names iPhone, iPad and Apple Vision under compatibility.

    Android

    Both apps run on Android. On Android, Snore Timeline brings the timeline, episodes, breathing flags, sleep stages and exports, but its Watch app is iOS only. SnoreLab bills itself as a snoring management app for iOS and Android.

    Export

    Snore Timeline exports a ZIP of CSV files (nightly summary, sleep stages, detected events, watch biometrics) plus episode clips or the full-night recording, and saves it to the Files app. Every column is listed in Export & sharing.

    In SnoreLab, export is a Premium feature. You can email a session report with the audio and chart attached, or export all sessions as a spreadsheet of numbers with no audio. On Android, the FAQ says, you can move audio to an SD card.

    Which one to pick

    Pick SnoreLab if

    • You’d like one number for snoring intensity. The Snore Score is benchmarked against the more than 170 million nights SnoreLab says it has monitored.
    • You put weight on a long track record of user reviews, and SnoreLab has 57,000 ratings at 4.7 on the App Store.

    Pick Snore Timeline if

    • You want no subscription, no in-app purchases and an App Store label that reads Data Not Collected.
    • You want to replay any moment of the night, and export CSV files and the full-night audio, at no cost. On SnoreLab, full-night recording and export are both Premium.
    • You want sleep stages without wearing a watch. Snore Timeline estimates them from the recording and draws them under your snoring on the same chart, while SnoreLab’s sleep phases come from an Apple Watch through Apple Health.
    • You want the app to nudge you when you snore, with a sound from the phone, a tap on your wrist or both. SnoreLab has no snore alarm [9].
    • You want to wake up in light sleep. Snore Timeline’s Smart Alarm picks the moment within a window you set. SnoreLab has no smart alarm. Its sleep phases come from an Apple Watch [2], and its alarm clock rings at the time you set [3].
    • You wear an Apple Watch and want an app on it, with live heart rate and HRV, wrist taps when you snore and for the alarm, and a stop button. SnoreLab has no Watch app.
    • You fall asleep with AirPods in and want them to record. SnoreLab’s FAQ says to disconnect Bluetooth audio devices [2].
    • You want sleep talking picked out on the timeline. SnoreLab’s FAQ sorts sounds into snoring levels and a gray noise category.

    Or run both on the same night. The FAQ below has the setup, and SnoreLab alternatives lists more options.

    Medical note

    Snore Timeline analyzes audio. It is not a medical device and does not diagnose sleep apnea or any other condition. Bring recordings to a doctor if you suspect breathing pauses.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is SnoreLab free?

    It’s a free download with a limited free tier. According to SnoreLab’s FAQ, the free version lets you record a night, see charts and stats in the morning, listen to captured audio, and tag remedies and factors. Once you’re past your first 5 sessions, it can’t run on 2 consecutive days.

    Premium lifts that limit. It adds full night recording, trends, export, audio backup, BreathFlow and the Breathing Disturbance Index, and the US App Store lists Premium purchases from $5.99 to $59.99.

    Does SnoreLab collect data?

    Yes, going by its own App Store label. As of 27 September 2026, SnoreLab lists Usage Data under Data Used to Track You, and Health & Fitness, Contact Info, User Content and Identifiers under Data Linked to You. Identifiers, Usage Data and Diagnostics sit under Data Not Linked to You.

    Its FAQ adds that recordings stay on your device and don’t upload to a server without your permission. The exception is Audio Backup. If you turn it on, SnoreLab stores encrypted audio on its server.

    Which app records sleep talking?

    Snore Timeline. It detects sleep talking as its own sound type and marks it in cream on the timeline, so you can tap it and play it back. SnoreLab records the room, but neither its site nor its App Store listing names sleep talking as something it detects or labels.

    Can I use both at once?

    Yes, with two phones, one per app. Set them side by side at the same distance from your head so both hear the same night. SnoreLab suggests arm’s length from your face, while Snore Timeline suggests 3 to 4 feet for snoring and 1 to 2 feet for breathing pauses. Pick one distance and use it for both.

    Plug both in and silence notifications on each. Any sound from one phone ends up in the other one’s recording, so turn off Snore Nudge in Snore Timeline that night too, or its tone will show up in SnoreLab’s audio.

    Expect the numbers to differ. Each app uses its own detection, and each phone has its own microphone.

    Which app works on Android?

    Both do. Snore Timeline runs on Android from Google Play and has the same timeline, episodes, breathing disruption flags, sleep stages and exports as the iPhone version. The Apple Watch app is the one piece that stays iOS only.

    SnoreLab describes itself as a snoring management app for iOS and Android, and its FAQ has Android-specific steps, like moving audio to an SD card.
