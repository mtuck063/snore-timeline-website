Can Your iPhone Detect Sleep Apnea?

What the iPhone does by itself, what an Apple Watch adds, and which apps record breathing patterns worth showing a doctor.

By Menelik Tucker · Updated 3 Oct 2026

Quick answer

No. An iPhone has no built-in sleep apnea feature, and no app can diagnose it. A clinician does that from a home sleep test or an in-lab sleep study. What your iPhone can do is collect patterns to show that clinician. Paired with an Apple Watch Series 9 or later, Ultra 2 or later, or SE 3, it shows nightly Breathing Disturbances in the Health app and can notify you of possible sleep apnea after a 30-day review. A microphone app such as Snore Timeline records the whole night and flags silences that end in a gasp or a loud breath, so you can play each one back.

Key takeaways

  • iOS has no sleep apnea feature that runs on the iPhone alone. Apple's sleep apnea notifications need a supported Apple Watch worn to bed at least 10 nights in a 30-day period.
  • Apple says the notifications aren't meant to diagnose sleep apnea, and that not everyone who has it will get one.
  • Microphone apps hear sound. They can flag silences, gasps and uneven breathing, and they miss events that make no noise.
  • Snore Timeline marks each breathing disruption on a timeline you can play back, rates them per hour, and adds time below 95% blood oxygen when a watch records it. It has no subscription or in-app purchases, and its App Store label reads Data Not Collected.
  • The American Academy of Sleep Medicine says a negative home sleep test can't rule sleep apnea out when your symptoms fit it. A quiet night in an app rules out even less.
Do not wait on an app

If you or a partner notice choking, gasping, or stopped breathing during sleep, talk to a clinician. Book that appointment now. You do not need a week of recordings first, and a quiet night in an app does not cancel what a partner saw.

    What an Apple Watch adds

    Apple's sleep apnea notifications run on the watch, and you read the results on the iPhone. According to Apple Support, the accelerometer picks up small wrist movements that go with interruptions in breathing, and each night gets marked Elevated or Not Elevated [1]. Every 30 days the watch reviews that data. If it sees Elevated breathing disturbances on a consistent basis, it sends a notification.

    What it needs

    • An Apple Watch Series 9 or later, Ultra 2 or later, or SE 3, on the latest watchOS, paired with an iPhone on the latest iOS.
    • At least 10 nights of wear in a 30-day period.
    • You must be 18 or older and not already diagnosed with sleep apnea.

    In the Health app, go to Browse, then Respiratory, and you'll find Breathing Disturbances and Sleep Apnea Notifications. You can export a PDF from there for your doctor [1]. Apple announced the feature with FDA 510(k) clearance in September 2024 [2].

    Apple is careful about the limits. Its support page says the feature isn't intended to diagnose, treat or aid in the management of sleep apnea, and that not everyone with sleep apnea will receive a notification [1]. So silence from the watch isn't an all-clear. It also records no sound, so you can't hear what happened on an Elevated night.     Apps that record breathing patterns

    A microphone app fills the gap the watch leaves. It records the night and marks the moments where your breathing went quiet and came back hard. When you compare them, four things matter: whether you can play back each flagged moment, whether the app counts events per night so you can follow a trend, whether you can export the recordings for a doctor, and what the app does with your data. I searched the App Store for sleep apnea and breathing apps on 3 October 2026 and describe each one below from its own listing or help center.

    Snore Timeline

    I built Snore Timeline. It flags a breathing disruption when it hears at least a minute of breathing, then 10 or more seconds of silence, then a recovery sound that breaks the silence, such as a loud snore, a gasp or a breath. Each one lands on the timeline as a gray-blue marker. Tap it in the nightly summary and you jump to that moment and hear the silence end.

    The summary grades every pause by length (under 12 seconds mild, 12 to 20 moderate, over 20 severe), calls out the longest one, and rates disruptions per hour from Minimal to Significant. Gasps get their own count. If you wear an Apple Watch that records blood oxygen, the same screen shows how much of the night you spent below 95% saturation and your lowest reading, and both come from the watch through Apple Health. The Breathing Disruptions page has the full rules.

    Patterns show up over weeks. The Weekly Summary compares this week with last, and the sleep factors let you tag nights with things like Alcohol, Slept on Side, Elevated Pillow or Mouth Taping. After 5 nights, with at least 2 tagged and 2 untagged, the app compares them, disruption counts included. It also estimates your sleep stages from the same audio, with no watch needed. CSV data, per-episode clips and full-night audio all export at no cost, and nothing leaves your phone unless you share it. The App Store label reads Data Not Collected, and there's no subscription or in-app purchase.

Its listing describes BreathFlow as a measure of breathing stability, correlated with apnea severity, that looks at sound patterns for signs of irregular breathing [6]. A Breathing Rate feature tracks your respiratory rate and picks out possible interruptions. Both sit in SnoreLab Premium, which costs $5.99 to $59.99. The listing says SnoreLab is not intended to diagnose or treat sleep apnea. Its privacy label declares Usage Data used to track you and Health & Fitness data linked to you.     Pillow

    Pillow's listing says it records audio events such as snoring, sleep apnea and sleep talking, and a recent release added a Breathing Disturbances section to track potential apnea and hypopnea events [14]. Its help center still says Pillow can't detect sleep apnea, and points to its sound recordings and Apple Watch breathing data as signs worth taking to a doctor [15]. Sound recordings are part of Premium, which costs $19.99 to $59.99. Snore Timeline records the full night at no cost.

    ShutEye

    ShutEye has 350K ratings on the US App Store. Its site says it listens for breathing changes along with snoring, sleep talking and coughing, and builds a sleep apnea risk report from your breathing and snoring patterns [12]. Plans run $9.99 to $69.99, and its label declares Identifiers used to track you [13]. Snore Timeline vs ShutEye compares the two.

    Sleep Cycle

    Sleep Cycle is a smart alarm and sleep tracker with 30K ratings on the US App Store. It listens through the iPhone microphone. Its help center lists breathing disruption data and respiratory rate as Premium, and says that data only appears when you start the session from the iPhone so sound tracking runs [7]. Premium costs $2.99 to $57.99, and its label declares Identifiers used to track you and Health & Fitness data linked to you [8]. Snore Timeline's breathing flags, playback and export carry no charge.     AutoSleep

    AutoSleep works from Apple Watch data and records no sound. Its listing describes sleep apnea tracking with a gauge, trend graphs and widgets, next to sleeping blood oxygen and respiration rate [16]. It costs $8.99 up front with no in-app purchases, and its label reads Data Not Collected. Pair it with a microphone app if you want to hear what happened on a bad night.

    SleepWatch ranks near the top of App Store searches for sleep apnea, with 328K ratings. Its listing doesn't describe an apnea feature. It logs sleep disruptions and sleeping blood oxygen from an Apple Watch and says not to rely on it for medical advice or diagnosis [17].

    What a pattern worth raising looks like

    One flagged night means little. Phones get knocked over, people roll away, a fan kicks on. A doctor learns more from what repeats. Look for:

    • Flags on most nights. A rate that stays in the same band across a week or two carries more weight than one bad night.
    • Silences that end in gasps. Play a few back. A long quiet stretch broken by a choking breath is what a partner describes when they say you stop breathing.
    • Clusters. A run of disruptions in one part of the night shows up as a tight group of markers on the timeline.
    • A link to position or alcohol. If tagged nights with alcohol, or nights on your back, carry more disruptions than untagged ones, note that.
    • Daytime signs. The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute lists daytime sleepiness, morning headaches and dry mouth among the symptoms, along with loud snoring and gasping at night [4].

    The count will run low. A microphone can't hear events that make no sound, like central apneas with no breathing effort or partial blockages that reduce airflow without a clean silence. Noise from a fan, a partner or a CPAP machine can cover quiet breathing. I checked Snore Timeline's flag against 57 overnight recordings from PSG-Audio, a public sleep-lab dataset with clinician-scored events [11], and it catches only some of the lab-scored events. The app has not been clinically validated. Research on phones points the same way. A 2022 meta-analysis of 11 smartphone studies found good pooled accuracy for moderate to severe sleep apnea, but with wide limits of agreement against a sleep study, and its authors caution against making clinical decisions from these devices [5]. Snoring apps that flag breathing pauses goes deeper into what a microphone can and can't hear.

    How sleep apnea gets diagnosed

    Most people start with a home sleep apnea test. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine describes it as equipment you wear in your own bed that measures your breathing and blood oxygen, and sometimes your heart rate [3]. A sleep team scores the results and a sleep doctor goes over them with you.

    A home test suits people who likely have moderate to severe sleep apnea and no other major medical conditions. The AASM says you shouldn't use one if your doctor suspects a different sleep disorder, or if you have lung disease, neuromuscular disease or congestive heart failure. Those cases call for an in-lab sleep study. It also says a negative home test can't rule sleep apnea out when your symptoms still fit, and more testing may follow [3].

    Some apps handle that step for you. Ognomy, for one, books video visits with sleep doctors and sends a home sleep test kit, and its listing says it takes most insurance [9]. That route ends in a diagnosis from a clinician.

    Bring what you've collected to that first appointment:

    • The PDF from the Health app, if your watch sent a notification.
    • A few clips of flagged pauses. Hearing one tells a doctor more than a number does.
    • The Detected Events export, which lists every disruption with its time, silence length and recovery strength. Export & Sharing shows how to get it.
    • Notes from a bed partner, and your own daytime symptoms.

    Setting up your iPhone tonight

    1. Place the phone. Put it 1 to 2 feet from your head, plugged in, with nothing over the microphone. That's closer than the 3 to 4 feet that works for snoring, because breathing is quieter.
    2. Quiet the room. Turn off fans and white noise if you can. They can cover the silence the app listens for.
    3. Wear the watch, if you have one. Turn on Sleep Tracking, then connect Apple Health in Snore Timeline under Settings, Health Integration. That brings in heart rate, respiratory rate and blood oxygen.
    4. Tag your nights. Mark Alcohol, Slept on Side or anything else you want to test. The Test a Remedy guide explains the comparison.
    5. Record a week or two. The nightly breathing assessment needs at least 2 hours of sleep, and the trend matters more than any single night.
    Medical note

    Snore Timeline analyzes audio. It is not a medical device and does not diagnose sleep apnea or any other condition. Bring recordings to a doctor if you suspect breathing pauses.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can my iPhone detect sleep apnea without an Apple Watch?

    Not by itself. iOS has no sleep apnea feature that runs on the phone alone. A microphone app can record your breathing all night and flag silences that end in a gasp or a loud breath, which gives you something to play for a doctor. No app diagnoses sleep apnea.

    Which apps show sleep apnea patterns?

    Apps that listen at night can show breathing patterns, though none of them diagnose. Snore Timeline flags each breathing disruption with audio you can replay, rates them per hour, and tracks the trend across weeks with no purchases. SnoreLab and Sleep Cycle put their breathing data in Premium, ShutEye gives a sleep apnea risk report, and Pillow has a Breathing Disturbances section. AutoSleep shows a sleep apnea gauge from Apple Watch data. Apple Watch sleep apnea notifications add a 30-day signal from wrist movement.

    How many nights should I record?

    Apple's notifications need the watch on for at least 10 nights in a 30-day period. For a microphone app, a week or two of full nights gives you a trend to look at. Snore Timeline's breathing assessment needs at least 2 hours of sleep in a night.

    Does a quiet night in an app mean I don't have sleep apnea?

    No. Audio apps miss events that make no sound, and Apple says not everyone with sleep apnea gets a notification. Even a negative home sleep test can't rule it out when symptoms fit, according to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine. If you snore loudly, gasp at night or feel sleepy during the day, see a clinician.

    Does Apple Watch blood oxygen show sleep apnea?

    Apple's sleep apnea notifications come from the accelerometer, per Apple Support. Blood oxygen readings are a separate feature, and they appear under Respiratory in the Health app. Snore Timeline reads them through Apple Health and shows how much of the night you spent below 95% and your lowest reading. A clinician has to interpret those numbers.

    Does Samsung's sleep apnea feature work with an iPhone?

    No. Samsung says its sleep apnea feature runs on a compatible Galaxy Watch paired with a Samsung Galaxy phone. It received FDA De Novo authorization in 2024 and checks for signs of moderate to severe obstructive sleep apnea over two nights of tracking within ten days, for adults 22 and older who haven't been diagnosed.
