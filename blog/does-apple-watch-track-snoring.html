Does Apple Watch Track Snoring? No. What It Does Instead

By Menelik Tucker · Updated 2 Oct 2026

Quick answer

No. Apple Watch has no snoring feature, and no app can turn it into an automatic snore tracker. On iPhone and Apple Watch, a recording has to start from an app that's open on screen, so nothing can start listening in the background after you fall asleep. Battery is the bigger limit. Recording and analyzing sound all night would drain the watch before morning. On its own, the watch estimates sleep stages and tracks your breathing rate, and on Series 9 or later, Ultra 2 or later, and SE 3, it uses the accelerometer to look for breathing disturbances that can point to sleep apnea over 30 days. For snoring, use an iPhone app that records through the phone's microphone. My app, Snore Timeline, doesn't need the watch at all. You start it with a tap, Siri or a bedtime shortcut, and it estimates your sleep stages from the recording and draws them under the snoring chart. Wear a watch and you add vitals, wrist taps when you snore, and a Watch app that can stop a recording but not start one.

Key takeaways

  • Apple's own sleep features don't detect snoring. They don't record sound at night either.
  • Overnight, the watch estimates REM, Core, and Deep sleep, gives you a sleep score, and logs breathing rate, heart rate, and wrist temperature.
  • Sleep apnea notifications run on the accelerometer, with no sound involved, and look at breathing disturbances over 30 days on Series 9 or later, Ultra 2 or later, and SE 3.
  • Apple says the feature is not intended to diagnose sleep apnea. Its Newsroom says it launched after FDA 510(k) clearance.
  • The snore recording itself happens on the iPhone. Snore Timeline estimates sleep stages from that audio alone and draws them under your snoring, so you can see which stage it falls in.
  • Pair Snore Timeline with a watch and you add heart rate, HRV, respiratory rate, blood oxygen, and wrist temperature, wrist taps for Snore Nudge and the alarm, and a Watch app that can stop a recording but not start one. If more than one device records your stages, each one's timeline stays separate so you can compare them.

    What Apple Watch measures at night

    Turn on Sleep Tracking and wear the watch to bed. It'll add sleep data to the Health app on your iPhone, and in the morning you get back what Apple's guide lists [1]:

    • Sleep stages. Estimates of how long you spent in REM, Core, and Deep sleep, and when you might have woken up.
    • Sleep score and trends. You get a score each night, and the Sleep app shows your trends over the past 14 days.
    • Breathing rate. The watch tracks your respiratory rate while you're asleep.
    • Heart rate and wrist temperature. Apple's guide links to both as overnight health data.

    watchOS 11 added the Vitals app. It pulls these overnight metrics into one view and can notify you when two or more of them land outside your typical range [5], and on watches that record blood oxygen, those readings go into Apple Health too, where third-party apps can read them if you give permission.

    Breathing disturbances

    One more metric shows up on Series 9 or later, Ultra 2 or later, and SE 3. It's called Breathing Disturbances. According to Apple's Newsroom, the accelerometer looks for small movements at the wrist that go with interruptions in normal breathing during sleep [5], and Apple Support says each night gets marked Elevated or Not Elevated [3]. Those readings feed the sleep apnea notification, which I get to further down.

    None of that is sound. The watch reads motion, heart signals, and temperature. It can't tell you when you snored, how loud it got, or what it sounded like.

    Why no app records your snoring on the watch

    Apple's own sleep features skip snoring [1][2][3], and its breathing disturbance metric comes from the accelerometer. The hardware isn't the problem. Apple Watch has a microphone, and the Noise app uses it to sample sound levels around you, though Apple says the watch doesn't record or save any sound to do that [14]. What stops a snoring app from using that microphone all night is the set of rules Apple gives third-party apps.

    Most people who look for snore detection on the watch want it to be automatic. They'd like to go to bed, fall asleep, and find their snoring logged in the morning, the way Apple's own sleep tracking works. Apple doesn't let a third-party app do that.

    • You have to start the recording. A watch app can only start a background session, a workout or audio session included, while it's open on screen [15]. On the iPhone, an app that's in the background can't start recording either. It gets an error unless the recording began while the app was open [18]. Apple's sleep tracking turns on by itself because it's part of the system. A snoring app has to come to the front on your phone each night, whether you tap record, ask Siri, or run a shortcut that opens it.
    • Battery, the biggest limit. Apple rates Series 11 for up to 24 hours and Ultra 3 for up to 42 [17]. Recording and analyzing sound all night would drain the watch before morning, with nothing left for the next day.
    • Only a workout keeps the microphone running all night. Lower your wrist and a watch app normally gets suspended. A workout session keeps it going, microphone included, until you end it [15]. Apple built workout sessions for exercise, though, so every night you record shows up as a workout in Apple Health and counts toward your Move and Exercise rings [16].
    • Placement. The watch sits on your wrist, often under the covers, and rubs against the bedding each time you turn over. A phone on the nightstand stays still, a foot or two from your face.

    So every snoring app that works with a watch splits the job. The iPhone records and detects. The watch adds vitals from your wrist or a vibration when you snore. I built Snore Timeline the same way. You start the recording on the iPhone with a tap, with Siri, or with a bedtime shortcut, and from there it runs on its own. The phone hears sustained snoring and sends a notification, iOS mirrors it to your watch as a tap, and the watch app doesn't have to run overnight at all.

    Pairing the watch with a snore app

    Snore Timeline

    Snore Timeline records through your iPhone microphone or AirPods. It detects snores, gasps, coughs, sleep talking, and breathing, and lays them out on a timeline you can scrub through and play back. Its Watch app comes bundled with the iOS app. From your wrist you can stop a recording, see your heart rate and HRV live during the session, and open a morning dashboard with your sleep score, recovery score, hypnogram, and vitals. The watch can't start a recording. That happens on the iPhone.

    Sleep stages don't need the watch. Snore Timeline estimates Awake, Light, Deep and REM from your breathing sounds, with the phone on the nightstand. On the nightly summary, the snoring chart switches between loudness and snores per minute, and a band of your sleep stages runs underneath it, so you can see whether your snoring clusters in light, deep or REM sleep. Tap a moment on the hypnogram and the snoring chart marks the same time. The sleep stages guide explains how the estimate works.

    Through Apple Health, the app reads six kinds of watch data: sleep stages, heart rate, HRV, respiratory rate, blood oxygen, and wrist temperature. (Wrist temperature needs a Series 8 or newer.) The watch delivers two kinds of wrist tap as well. Snore Nudge taps you when the app hears sustained snoring, and the wake-up alarm can use taps in place of sound to wake you. Oura, Whoop, Garmin, Ultrahuman, or any other wearable that writes sleep data to Apple Health feeds the same sleep score, recovery score and hypnogram. When a night has stages from more than one source, each source's timeline stays separate, so you can compare them instead of getting a blend. The Smart Wake Up Alarm wakes you in light sleep within a 15, 30 or 45 minute window you set, with or without the watch. There's no subscription and there are no in-app purchases, and the App Store label reads Data Not Collected. The Apple Watch support page has the details.

    Koala Nap

    Koala Nap's App Store listing describes gentle alerts through Apple Watch, iPhone, or Bluetooth earphones that nudge you toward a quieter sleep position [6], and says the app detects snoring with machine learning and works with common bedroom background noise. The watch is one alert channel out of three, and the listing names Apple Watch under compatibility. You can use Koala Nap without one. Its label declares User Content and Diagnostics as data not linked to you. Snore Timeline's answer is Snore Nudge, which plays a tone from the iPhone, taps your wrist, or both, and makes the tone a step louder each time a nudge doesn't help.

    Pillow

    With Pillow, according to its App Store listing, you wear your Apple Watch to bed and the app tracks your sleep from it, with stages (Awake, REM, Light, Deep), detailed heart rate analysis, and last night's report right on the watch [7]. It also mentions recording snoring and sleep talking as audio events. There's a manual mode next to the automatic one, and without a watch, Pillow's help center says the iPhone estimates stages from movement and sound [9]. The listing puts heart rate analysis, sound recordings, Smart Wake-up and CSV export under Premium [7]. Snore Timeline includes its Smart Wake Up Alarm, full-night audio and export at no cost. Pillow has an App Store Editors' Choice badge and 96K ratings. Snore Timeline vs Pillow puts the two side by side.

    Snollo

    Snollo's App Store listing says it monitors heart rate and respiratory rate through the night from your watch, and tracks blood oxygen too. All acoustic analysis happens on the iPhone, per the listing, and raw audio never goes to external servers. Without a watch, its site says, sleep stages come from the iPhone's microphone and motion sensors, and the phone has to lie on the bed near you [11]. It has no Watch app. The listing names iPhone, Mac and Apple Vision under compatibility [10].

    SnoreLab

    If your iOS device is paired to an Apple Watch, SnoreLab's FAQ says you can sync SnoreLab with Apple Health, and sleep phases then appear in your session chart [8]. That's its only source of sleep phases. SnoreLab has no Watch app, and its App Store listing names iPhone, iPad and Apple Vision under compatibility [12]. It has no snore alarm to tap your wrist either [13]. BreathFlow, its breathing score, comes from the phone's microphone. Apple Health data doesn't feed into it, according to the FAQ.

    SnoreLab's answer to a nightly number is the Snore Score, which measures snoring intensity, and it lets you track remedies. Snore Timeline's answer is a 0 to 100 sleep score for the whole night, where snoring and breathing disruptions count against it. It tracks remedies too, with 23 built-in sleep factors plus any you create, and after 5 tracked nights (at least 2 tagged and 2 untagged) it compares them and ranks what helped. SnoreLab has 57K ratings, far more than my app. Snore Timeline reached the App Store in September 2025, SnoreLab in 2012 and Pillow in 2014, and I build it alone, with no outside funding and no marketing budget. The Snore Timeline vs SnoreLab page compares them in depth.

    Apple Watch sleep apnea notifications vs an audio breathing-pause flag

    Both deal with breathing in your sleep. The signals they use and the claims they make are different.

    Snore Timeline breathing disruption flagSound from the iPhone micEach nightTimestamped events you can play backNot a medical device
    Apple Watch sleep apnea notificationsWrist movement from the accelerometer30-day evaluationA notification and a PDF of breathing disturbance dataFDA 510(k) cleared, per Apple

    What Apple's feature does

    Every 30 days the watch analyzes its breathing disturbance data, and if it sees consistent signs of moderate to severe sleep apnea, it notifies you [4]. Along with the time period involved and some educational material, the notification includes a PDF of three months of breathing disturbance data to share with a doctor [5]. Apple's Newsroom says the feature became available following FDA 510(k) clearance and other health authority authorizations, in more than 150 countries and regions [5]. It's meant for people 18 or older who have not been diagnosed with sleep apnea. Apple states that it is not intended to diagnose, treat, or aid in the management of sleep apnea [2][3].

    What an audio flag does

    The breathing disruption flag in Snore Timeline waits for a sequence. First comes at least a minute of audible breathing. Then 10 or more seconds of silence. Then a recovery sound, like a loud snore, gasp or breath, ends the quiet. Every flag sits on the timeline with the audio around it, so you can hear the pause and the gasp that ends it. The app is not a medical device. It makes no sleep apnea claim.

    You can run both at once. The watch gives you a monthly signal based on movement, and the phone gives you nightly recordings of the sounds that go with it. These features do not diagnose sleep apnea; a sleep study does. If either one worries you, bring the notification PDF and your recordings to a doctor, and see Gathering breathing data to show your doctor for what to bring.

    Setting it up tonight

    To pair Snore Timeline with your watch, follow these steps. The watch app only runs on iOS.

    1. Charge the watch. If the battery is under 30 percent before bed, Apple prompts you to charge it [1]. Then turn on Sleep Tracking in the Sleep app. That's what makes the watch write stages to Apple Health.
    2. Connect Apple Health. In Snore Timeline, go to Settings and find Health Integration. Turn on the Apple Health toggle and approve the permissions sheet. The first sync goes back up to two years.
    3. Turn on Snore Nudge. Under Detection in Settings, switch on Snore Nudge and set Nudge with to Wrist tap. Pick Both if you'd like the tone as well. The app will ask for notification permission. That's because the taps arrive as mirrored notifications.
    4. Test it. Tap Test Nudge and lock your iPhone. The taps should follow a few seconds later. Notifications only mirror to the watch while the phone is locked.
    5. Make the taps firmer. In the iPhone's Watch app, set Sounds & Haptics to Prominent.
    6. Set a silent alarm. Under Wake Up, turn on Wrist nudge on Apple Watch. If you add the Smart Wake Up Alarm, it rings in light sleep inside a 15, 30 or 45 minute window you pick. It needs Sleep Insights on.
    7. Place the phone and start. Put the iPhone 1 to 2 feet from your head, plugged in and face-down. Tap the record button on the phone.

    Keep the watch on your wrist, because one left on its charger still counts as paired, and with the Wrist tap setting you'd get no nudge at all. In the morning, Apple Watch needs 1 to 3 hours to finalize its sleep stages, and Snore Timeline syncs them in the background once they land. The getting started guide covers the first recording in more detail.

    Medical note

    Snore Timeline analyzes audio. It is not a medical device and does not diagnose sleep apnea or any other condition. Bring recordings to a doctor if you suspect breathing pauses.

    Frequently asked questions

    Does Apple Watch record snoring?

    No. Apple's sleep tracking guide covers sleep stages, sleep score, breathing rate, heart rate, and wrist temperature. Snoring, sound and the microphone don't come up anywhere in it.

    To record snoring you need an app on your iPhone. Snore Timeline uses the iPhone microphone or AirPods. Every snore lands on a timeline for playback.

    Can Apple Watch detect sleep apnea?

    Apple Watch Series 9 or later, Ultra 2 or later, and SE 3 can send sleep apnea notifications. Using its accelerometer, the watch looks for breathing disturbances, and over a 30-day evaluation period it can notify you of signs of moderate to severe sleep apnea.

    Apple states that the feature is not intended to diagnose, treat, or aid in the management of sleep apnea, and that it's meant for adults 18 or older who have not been diagnosed with it. If you get a notification, talk to a doctor. They may order a sleep study.

    Which snoring apps work with Apple Watch?

    Snore Timeline comes with its own Watch app, which shows live heart rate and HRV, taps your wrist when you snore, and can stop a recording but not start one. Through Apple Health it also reads heart rate, HRV, respiratory rate, blood oxygen, and wrist temperature. Koala Nap and Pillow list Apple Watch apps of their own on the App Store. Snollo reads watch vitals through Apple Health, and SnoreLab's FAQ says it can show sleep phases from Apple Health when your iPhone is paired with a watch. Neither Snollo nor SnoreLab lists Apple Watch under compatibility on the App Store.

    The snoring audio always comes from the phone app. What the watch adds is vitals or a vibration.

    Do I need an Apple Watch to track snoring?

    No. Your iPhone's microphone can record snoring by itself. Snore Timeline works from audio alone. It estimates sleep stages from your breathing and draws them under the snoring chart, so you can see which stage your snoring falls in. Adding a watch gets you wrist vitals, and Snore Nudge can then tap your wrist in place of a tone.

    Does Snore Timeline vibrate the watch when I snore?

    Yes, if you turn on Snore Nudge. It's in Settings under Detection. Set Nudge with to Wrist tap or Both. Once the app confirms sustained snoring, the watch gives three short taps so you shift position.

    The taps come through Apple Watch notification mirroring. They work with your iPhone locked and the watch app closed. The watch does have to be on your wrist, though, and you'll see each nudge and what happened after it in the morning summary.
