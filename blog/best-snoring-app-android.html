Best Snoring Apps for Android: Free Apps and What They Collect

By Menelik Tucker · Updated 28 Sep 2026

Quick answer

Two of the Android snoring apps here declare no data collected and no data shared in their Google Play Data safety sections, Snore Timeline and SnoreClock. Snore Timeline has no in-app purchases. It records the whole night, lets you replay and export any of it, flags breathing pauses, and estimates sleep stages from the phone’s audio alone, with no watch. SnoreClock sells a Plus subscription. SnoreLab, Sleep as Android, Sleep Cycle and ShutEye have larger user bases. All four declare that they may collect some data. Samsung Health detects snoring only when a Galaxy Watch or Galaxy Fit3 is connected to your phone.

Key takeaways

  • Snore Timeline and SnoreClock both declare in Google Play's Data safety section that they collect no data and share none with third parties.
  • Snore Timeline's Play listing shows no in-app purchases. SnoreLab, SnoreClock, Sleep as Android, Sleep Cycle and ShutEye all carry the In-app purchases tag.
  • SnoreLogic says every feature is free with no ads. It also declares that it may collect personal info, audio and three other data types.
  • Samsung Health only detects snoring with a Galaxy Watch or Galaxy Fit3 connected to your phone. The phone does the recording.
  • Snore Timeline estimates sleep stages from the phone’s audio. SnoreLab’s FAQ says its sleep phases show only on iPhones paired with an Apple Watch, and it points people who want sleep tracking and a smart alarm to its sister app, Sleepwave.
  • Snore Timeline on Android writes your sleep session, stages and respiratory rate to Health Connect. Its Apple Watch app, Siri support and Apple Health stay on iPhone.

According to Google's help page, even an independent security review doesn't verify that a developer's Data safety disclosure is accurate and complete.

    Snollo and Pillow, two iPhone apps you may have seen, have no Google Play listing, and when I searched Play for each on the same date, the results were apps from other developers. My guide to free snoring apps for iPhone covers both.

    Snore Timeline: every feature included, no data collected

    On Android, Snore Timeline records the whole night through your phone's microphone or a Bluetooth mic, and a sound classifier on the phone labels snoring, gasps, coughs, sneezes, sleep talking and breathing, with each detection landing on a zoomable timeline. Snores close together form episodes. A 30-second gap starts a new one. Once it has heard a minute of audible breathing, the app flags any silent stretch of 10 seconds or more that ends in a recovery sound.

    It estimates sleep stages (Awake, Light, Deep, REM) from the recording alone, so you don't need a watch, and each night gets a score out of 100. You can tag nights with any of 23 sleep factors, such as Mouth Guard, Nasal Strips or Slept on Side, and after 5 tracked nights, at least 2 tagged and 2 untagged, the app compares them and ranks what helped. Replay any moment and export the full night or single episodes at no cost. With Health Connect turned on, it writes your sleep session, stages and respiratory rate there for other apps to read.

    Its Play listing shows no in-app purchases and a Data safety section that declares no data collected and none shared, alongside 4.9 stars from 41 reviews and 1K+ downloads. That review base is small next to the apps below. Snore Timeline reached the App Store in September 2025, while SnoreLab has been around since 2012, and I build it alone, with no outside funding and no marketing budget. Weigh this entry with that in mind. The Android support page lists what the app includes.

    SnoreLab: the largest snore-specific user base

    SnoreLab's Play listing shows 4.4 stars from 52.6K reviews and 5M+ downloads, and it describes a Snore Score for snoring intensity, BreathFlow (which it calls a measure of breathing stability), breathing-rate tracking, full-night recording and emailing sound files. The remedy and lifestyle tracking stands out. Log a mouthguard, a nasal strip or a drink before bed, and you can see how each night compares, much as Snore Timeline does with its sleep factors.

    Its FAQ fills in what the listing leaves out. After your first 5 sessions, the free version can’t run on 2 consecutive days, and full-night recording and export are Premium. Sleep phases appear only on iPhones paired with an Apple Watch, and for sleep tracking and a smart alarm the FAQ points you to SnoreLab’s sister app, Sleepwave. It also tells you to disconnect Bluetooth audio devices before recording.

    The Play header carries the Contains ads and In-app purchases tags. Its Data safety section says no data is shared with third parties and lists personal info, app activity and 2 other data types the app may collect. Data is encrypted in transit, and you can ask for deletion. My Snore Timeline vs SnoreLab comparison goes feature by feature.

    SnoreClock: a plain recorder with a clean Data safety entry

    SnoreClock by Ralph Schiffhauer does one job. You put the phone by the bed, press the red button and sleep, and in the morning red bars mark likely snoring on a volume graph. The listing says it records all sounds for up to 11 hours, works in background mode, and helps you check whether you snore, your partner snores or you talk in your sleep.

    The listing describes a Plus version, sold as a subscription, that removes ads and adds recording to an SD card, a sound or vibration alert on snoring, audio file sharing and statistics backup. SnoreClock shows 4.6 stars from 8.33K reviews and 1M+ downloads. Its Data safety section matches Snore Timeline's, with no data collected and none shared with third parties, so if you want a bare recorder and don't mind paying for alerts and sharing, it's a good fit. Snore Timeline’s audio exports are free.

    Sleep as Android: a configurable smart alarm, with snoring as one feature

    Sleep as Android by Urbandroid is a smart alarm first. Its listing confirms AI-powered sound recognition for snoring (its anti-snoring feature), sleep talk and sickness, and lists sonar contactless tracking, sleep respiration analysis with a low breath rate alarm, and a long run of wearables, from Pixel Watch and Galaxy watches to Garmin, Polar and Fitbit. It has Google's Editors' Choice badge. Ratings sit at 4.6 stars from 392K reviews.

    You get 7 days of premium. After that, keep the freemium version or upgrade. The header carries the Contains ads and In-app purchases tags. Data safety says the app may share Location, Health and fitness and 2 other data types, and may collect Location, Health and fitness and 3 others, with data encrypted in transit. It also says data can’t be deleted. Urbandroid’s privacy policy says health data from Health Connect is used only for features you turn on, and that it doesn’t sell user data. If you back up to its SleepCloud service, your sleep records, including sleep cycles, deep sleep percentage, snoring percentage, noise levels and city-level location, are stored anonymously, and you can delete them from SleepCloud’s privacy tab. Ads are served through Facebook Audience Network using your device’s advertising ID, which you can switch off in the app’s privacy settings.

    ShutEye: sleep sounds with snore detection

    ShutEye by Enerjoy pairs a sleep tracker with a large bedtime library of sleep stories, white noise, nature sounds and guided meditation, and for snoring, the listing says it detects and analyzes it through the night and lets you review snoring recordings and sleep events. It tracks light, deep and REM stages. There's a daily sleep score and a smart alarm that wakes you during your lightest stage. Snore Timeline’s stages and sleep score come from the recording too. It shows 4.4 stars from 109K reviews and 10M+ downloads.

    It sells in-app purchases. Data safety says the app may share Personal info, App activity and 2 other data types with third parties and may collect App activity, and that data is encrypted in transit with deletion available on request. ShutEye makes most sense when getting to sleep is the hard part. Snoring is a side question.

    Sleep Cycle: smart alarm first, with a snore recorder

    Sleep Cycle built its name on the smart alarm. The Android listing still leads with wake-up windows of up to 90 minutes, puts its snore recorder and sleep talk recorder alongside, and says it can tell whether you or a partner is snoring. It also runs on Wear OS. It shows 4.3 stars from 218K reviews and 10M+ downloads.

    The header carries the In-app purchases tag. Its Data safety section says it may share Location, Personal info and 3 other data types with third parties, and may collect Personal info, Health and fitness and 3 others. Data is encrypted in transit and you can request deletion. Sleep Cycle’s privacy policy says health data stays on your device by default, that its optional backup sends everything in the app to its servers on Google Cloud, and that with your consent it can share personal data with research studies. If you share a bed, its Who's Snoring? feature is the reason to pick it, since it tells your snores from a partner's.

    SnoreLogic: free with no ads, declares some collection

    The Play package com.passionforlife.snore is listed as SnoreLogic: Track Your Snoring, from Passion for Life Healthcare (UK) Ltd, and the listing says the app and all of its features are completely free to use, with no ads or pop-ups. No in-app purchases show in the header. It reports snoring frequency and volume, a Breathing Disruption Index that counts times you may have stopped or struggled to breathe, remedy logging and a setup programme for the company's own mouthpiece. It shows 3.2 stars from 186 reviews and 100K+ downloads.

    Its Data safety section says nothing is shared with third parties, and it lists personal info, audio and 3 other data types the app may collect, with encryption in transit and an option to request deletion. Audio on that list means the developer declared it collects audio. The declaration alone doesn't show where the audio goes.

    Samsung Health: snore detection if you wear a Galaxy Watch

    Samsung's support page describes snore detection as a Samsung Health feature that needs a Wear OS Galaxy Watch, or a Galaxy Fit3, connected to your phone through the Galaxy Wearable app. The phone does the listening. You turn it on in Samsung Health under Sleep, Advanced measurement, Snoring, and a pop-up warns that it increases phone battery use. You pick whether to detect snoring Always or Once, can switch on Record audio with the phone near your head, and can set recordings to delete after 7, 31 or 100 days.

    Samsung Health on Google Play has 1B+ downloads. Its rating is 3.1 stars from 1.58M reviews. Its Data safety section says it may share Location, Personal info and 2 other data types, and may collect Location, Personal info and 8 others. The page gives no route without a compatible wearable.

    Placement and battery on Android

    An overnight recording runs for hours with the screen off. Android's power management can end it early. I took these steps from Snore Timeline's Android support page. Most apply to any recorder on this list.

    • Remove the battery restriction. When Snore Timeline shows the "Improve Recording Reliability" dialog, tap Allow and confirm, or if you already tapped "Not Now," grant it later under battery in your phone's app settings.
    • Leave the recording notification alone. The notification reads "Recording" with the elapsed time. Dismissing it can cut the night short. So can blocking the app's notifications.
    • Plug the phone in. The app does record on battery. Charging removes any reason for Android to close it.
    • Place it face-down, 30 to 60 cm from your head. At that distance your breathing stays audible. Breathing disruption detection and sleep stages both depend on it.
    • Check the manufacturer's own power layer. If recordings stop overnight, set Snore Timeline to unrestricted in your brand's per-app battery or background setting.

    A phone call can take the microphone. The app then tries to resume. You can also set a recording delay of up to 180 minutes in 5-minute steps, and nothing during the delay is saved. Troubleshooting covers recordings that still cut out.

    Reading your first morning

    Open last night from the calendar. The timeline draws the night as a waveform you can zoom from 1-second detail out to a 1-hour overview, with readings in approximate dB SPL. Each detection sits where it happened. Tap any moment to play it back.

    Start with episodes. Snores grouped together count as one episode until a 30-second gap closes it, which tells you whether you snored in long runs or short bursts. Then look for breathing disruption markers. Each one means the app heard at least a minute of breathing, then 10 or more seconds of silence, then a recovery sound. Give it a few nights before you draw conclusions. Breathing Disruptions explains what the flag does and doesn't mean.

    The hypnogram estimates awake, light, deep and REM stages from your breathing and movement sounds, and the sleep score out of 100 comes with sleep efficiency, wake after sleep onset and respiratory rate. The weekly view compares nights.

    Sharing with a doctor

    The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute lists breathing that starts and stops, frequent loud snoring and gasping for air as symptoms of sleep apnea during sleep, and notes you may not know you have them until someone tells you. A recording lets you hear them yourself.

    On Android, Snore Timeline saves exported audio to your Downloads folder, with full-night files under Downloads > Exported Recordings and single episode clips under Downloads > Snoring Episodes, each in a dated folder. The Export Report sheet bundles summaries, sleep phases, events and optional audio for a date range, and you can share it from the app. Export & Sharing walks through the report. For why several nights beat one, see Gathering Breathing Data to Show Your Doctor.

    Any app on this list can help you spot a pattern, but none of them can tell you whether you have sleep apnea; for that you need a doctor.

    Medical note

    Snore Timeline analyzes audio. It is not a medical device and does not diagnose sleep apnea or any other condition. Bring recordings to a doctor if you suspect breathing pauses.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is there a free snoring app for Android?

    Yes. Snore Timeline is free on Google Play, has no in-app purchases, and declares no data collected in its Data safety section. SnoreLogic's listing also says all its features are free with no ads, though it declares that it may collect personal info, audio and three other data types.

    The rest are free to download and sell upgrades.

    Does Samsung Health detect snoring?

    Yes, with a wearable. Samsung's support page says snore detection needs a Wear OS Galaxy Watch, or a Galaxy Fit3, connected to your phone through the Galaxy Wearable app. The phone does the recording. It has to sit near your head.

    Does SnoreLab work on Android?

    Yes. Its Google Play listing shows 4.4 stars from 52.6K reviews and 5M+ downloads, with ads and in-app purchases. Its Data safety section says it shares no data with third parties and may collect personal info, app activity and two other data types.

    Which Android snoring app collects the least data?

    Going by Google Play's Data safety declarations on 27 September 2026, two apps in this comparison declare "No data collected" and "No data shared with third parties": Snore Timeline and SnoreClock. Every other app here declares that it may collect at least one data type.

    These are developer declarations. Google notes that even an independent security review of an app doesn't verify that its Data safety disclosure is accurate and complete.

    Does Snore Timeline on Android have every iPhone feature?

    No. Per the Android support page, Apple Watch and wearable support, Siri and Apple Shortcuts, Apple Health and home-screen widgets are iPhone-only. Android writes the sleep session, stages and respiratory rate to Health Connect instead.

    Android adds app shortcuts, a bedtime reminder, a recording delay and a wake alarm, while detection, the timeline, episodes, breathing disruption flags, sleep stages and exports work on both.
