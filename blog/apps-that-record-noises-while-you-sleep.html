Apps That Record Noises While You Sleep (2026)

By Menelik Tucker · Updated 30 Oct 2026

Quick answer

An app that records noises while you sleep keeps the microphone open all night and either saves everything or saves a clip each time its classifier recognizes a sound. If you don’t know what the noise is yet, pick a whole-night recorder, because a clip app keeps only the sounds it can name. I make Snore Timeline. It records the whole night on iPhone and Android and labels snoring, gasps, coughs, sleep talking, laughter and breathing pauses. It marks anything it couldn’t name so you can play it. It costs nothing, and the audio stays on your phone. This advice draws on 2,656 recent App Store reviews of nine recorders.

Key takeaways

  • Clips lose sounds: most recorders save a short clip only when a sound triggers them, so a sound the classifier can’t label may never be saved. SnoreLab saves only samples unless you pay [13] [17].
  • Reviews: in 2,656 recent App Store reviews of nine recorders, the subscription wall is the top complaint. A fan is the most common false trigger.
  • Prevalence: 40 percent of adult men and 24 percent of women snore habitually. Between 60 and 65 percent of people have talked in their sleep [1] [2].
  • The gasp: the AASM lists snoring, then a silent pause, then a gasp as a sign of sleep apnea. A recording of it is something to show a doctor. It diagnoses nothing [8].
  • Screen locked: an iPhone app keeps recording with the screen locked only if its developer turned on Apple’s background audio mode [11]. Two apps here tell you to leave them open on screen all night [19] [23].

    Whole night or clips: the choice that decides what you’ll hear

    Every app on this page listens all night. The difference is what it keeps. Most keep clips because clips make small files. A whole-night recorder keeps every minute and puts its labels on top of the full recording.

A clip app can miss a groan on the out-breath, a cat on the dresser, or whatever your partner swears you do at 3 a.m.

    How to work out what a noise was

    This is what I do with my own nights. On the timeline, the waveform brightens as soon as a sound starts, about a second before a label can appear. Apricot is the color for a sound the app counted but couldn’t name. So a bright apricot swell often marks a sound with no label, and those hold the surprises.

    1. Play the apricot swells first. Zoom in all the way. The swells mark sounds without a name, such as a door, a car or a groan.
    2. Check the rhythm. Snoring and groaning follow your breathing, one sound per breath, in a long run. A thump or a bark has no rhythm. Zoom in to the 1-second bars to see it.
    3. Check the clock. The time sits under the center line. If a sound at 2:14 a.m. lines up with the heating, the cat door or a bathroom trip, you have your answer.
    4. Read the frequency color. The shade of orange shows pitch. Dark orange is low rumble, 50 to 250 Hz, where snoring sits. Bright orange is high pitch, like the “s” sounds of speech or a hiss. A sound that’s all high pitch isn’t a snore.
    5. Listen with the room in it. Playback starts about two seconds before the sound, so you hear the room around it. The app gets things wrong sometimes. When a blanket muffled a sound or two sounds overlapped, your ears settle it.
    6. Transcribe the words. If it’s speech, on-device transcription writes it out, and the audio never leaves the phone. It works on nights recorded at Standard or High Fidelity. Basic, the lowest quality tier, cuts too much of the speech range.

    Two nights beat one. A sound that comes back at the same time each night tells you more than one clip. So does a sound that shows up only on nights you tag with a factor, like wine, a late meal or a cold. The remedy guide covers tagging.

    The apps, one by one

    Snore Timeline

    Snore Timeline records every minute of the night. It estimates sleep stages from your breathing and gives each night a sleep score. It also has an Apple Watch app, Snore Nudge (a sound, an iPhone vibration or a wrist tap when you snore) and a Smart Wake Up Alarm.

    • Sounds it names: snoring, gasps, coughs, sleep talking, laughter and audible breathing disruptions. Loud Sound catches anything else above a volume threshold. On the Automatic setting, the app re-measures the room every minute and keeps the threshold just above it.
    • Free tier: every feature. That includes three quality tiers, a 1 to 8 GB storage cap, an Episode-Only mode that trims silence, on-device transcription, and export of the full night, clips or a CSV.
    • Price: free, with no subscription or in-app purchases.
    • Privacy label: Data Not Collected. There’s no account, server or analytics.
    • Reviews: I left my own app out of the review sample.

    Apple Voice Memos

    Voice Memos is already on your iPhone. Tap record and put the phone down. You get one file with every sound, eight hours to scrub through by hand. It stops if another app plays audio [10].

    • Sounds it names: none. It has no labels, event list or counts.
    • Free tier: every feature. Skip Silence jumps over the gaps, Wide Spectrum keeps background sounds in, and iCloud syncs the file [28].
    • Price: None.
    • Privacy label: Data Linked to You. The Audio Data it declares is for iCloud sync.
    • Reviews: not in my review sample.

    ShutEye

    ShutEye brings 350,000 ratings and a library of more than 70 sounds. Its blog says its AI analyzes the files the microphone records, and that you can play back and share selected recordings [16].

    • Sounds it names: snoring and sleep talk, saved as clips [24].
    • Free tier: I found nothing that says whether free users can play clips or how long clips are kept.
    • Price: Premium, $4.99 to $69.99.
    • Privacy label: Data Used to Track You. Its FAQ covers billing and says personal information is stored on encrypted servers.
    • Reviews: 51 of its 150 most recent reviews, from 17 April to 6 October 2026, mention the subscription.

    Pillow

    Pillow’s strength is its Apple Watch tracking, and it carries an Editors’ Choice badge. Its listing says it records “important audio events” on the device [25].

    • Sounds it names: snoring, sleep apnea and sleep talking [25]. I’d read “sleep apnea” there as a sound label and nothing more.
    • Free tier: no sound recordings or CSV export. Its help center puts both in Premium [15].
    • Price: Premium, $19.99 a month, $59.99 a quarter or $49.99 a year.
    • Privacy label: Data Linked to You.
    • Reviews: 62 of its 150 most recent reviews, from 27 March to 7 October 2026, mention the subscription. One, from April, says it only ever caught the dog snoring.

    SnoreLab

    SnoreLab has 57K ratings, a Snore Score and remedy tracking. Snore Timeline covers the same ground with its sleep score and 23 built-in sleep factors you can tag. SnoreLab suits people whose only question is snoring.

    • Sounds it names: snoring. Other sounds show as gray noise bars, and its FAQ doesn’t list sleep talking [13].
    • Free tier: samples, with a limit on how many you can play each night. The full night and export are Premium.
    • Price: Premium, $5.99 to $59.99.
    • Privacy label: Data Used to Track You. Its FAQ describes a Premium backup that stores encrypted recordings on SnoreLab’s server.
    • Reviews: in its 150 most recent reviews, from 19 December 2025 to 8 October 2026, health worry is the top reason for recording, at 30 [26].

    Sleep Cycle

    Sleep Cycle is a smart alarm first. Its feature page says it can tell your snoring from your partner’s and name pet, household and neighbor noise [31].

    • Sounds it names: snoring, coughing and sleep talking, as clips [27]. It doesn’t offer whole-night recording.
    • Free tier: no sound clips. Its help center lists snore and sound detection under Premium [14].
    • Price: Premium, $2.99 to $57.99.
    • Privacy label: Data Used to Track You.
    • Reviews: not in my sample.

    Noxogy

    Noxogy is Prime Sleep Recorder under a new name. It saves a clip when a sound passes the sensitivity you set. It also offers a snoring-only mode, a graph of room noise, CSV export of your history and iCloud sync [20]. Reviews show the trigger has behaved differently from version to version.

    • Free tier: reviews from 2025 onward say most clips sit behind the subscription.
    • Price: Full Version, $5.99 a month or $23.99 once. If you buy, pick the one-time price.
    • Privacy label: Data Linked to You.
    • Reviews: its 500 most recent reviews, from May 2016 to September 2026, describe clips cut short and whole nights of fan noise. In July 2024, one reviewer suspected the app added sounds to its free preview to sell the upgrade.

    Snore Recorder - Sleep Monitor

    Snore Recorder - Sleep Monitor saves clips and asks you to leave it open on screen [23]. Its listing advertises “AI sleep apnea detection”, a medical claim I’d treat with care.

    • Sounds it names: snoring, talking and shouting [23].
    • Free tier: every clip plays.
    • Price: free. It lists no in-app purchases.
    • Privacy label: Data Not Linked to You. It declares Audio Data.
    • Reviews: its 488 most recent reviews, from September 2025 to October 2026, are mostly short four- and five-star notes.

    Dream Talk Recorder

    Dream Talk Recorder records only when a sound triggers it, and its listing says to leave the app open on screen [19].

    • Sounds it names: talking and snoring.
    • Free tier: up to two hours of recording.
    • Price: Full Version, $4.99 once.
    • Privacy label: Data Used to Track You.
    • Reviews: 4 of its 142 reviews, from September 2011 to September 2026, are about false triggers.

    Sleep Talk Recorder

    Sleep Talk Recorder, from 2010, is the oldest app here built for sleep talk. It sorts clips on a night-by-night timeline, and users can share clips to a public top list [18].

    • Sounds it names: a filter starts recording when it hears speech. It sorts clips into talking, snoring or unknown.
    • Free tier: you can play back some nights. The rest need the unlock.
    • Price: “Unlock all nights”, $3.99 to $29.99.
    • Privacy label: Data Not Linked to You.
    • Reviews: 170 of its 500 most recent reviews, from February 2015 to September 2026, are one-star. Most of those complain about fan noise or the unlock.

    Set up a night that catches everything

    1. Plug it in on the nightstand, never under a pillow. Recording uses 5 to 7 percent of battery an hour. Heat shortens battery life and pauses charging [12].
    2. Turn on Loud Sound Detection. It’s off by default, and it catches loud sounds the app can’t name. Leave the threshold on Automatic, which keeps it just above your room’s background noise. Or switch to Fixed and set it a few dB above your quiet room’s reading. Lower catches more, including rustling.
    3. Pick the tier for the sound. Basic (8 MB an hour) keeps the snoring range. Standard (15 MB an hour) keeps speech, up to 8,000 Hz. Only High Fidelity (25 MB an hour) keeps breathing audible.
    4. Place it for the sound you suspect. Put it three to four feet away for snoring, and one to two feet away for breathing and speech. Keep the microphone uncovered.
    5. Quiet the fan if you can. A fan creates no false events in Snore Timeline, but it can drown out quiet sounds. If the fan stays on, move the phone closer and raise the sensitivity.
    6. Cap the storage. A 2 GB limit holds one to two weeks at high quality. When it fills, the app deletes the oldest nights first. With History on, it deletes only their audio and keeps the episodes and timeline.
    7. Tag the night. Tag each night with factors like alcohol, caffeine, a cold or a late meal. Once you have 5 recorded nights, the app compares them and ranks what changed. Each factor gets a result after 3 recorded nights with it and 3 without.
    Medical note

    Snore Timeline analyzes audio on your phone. It is not a medical device and does not diagnose sleep apnea, bruxism, REM sleep behavior disorder or any other condition. Its sleep stages are estimates. If a recording holds gasping, choking, long silences between breaths, shouting or violent movement, take it to a doctor.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is the best app to record noises while you sleep?

    If you don’t know the noise yet, start with an app that keeps the whole night. The at a glance table shows which apps do, and which let you play it back without paying.

    Is there a free app that records you sleeping?

    Yes. Voice Memos comes with the iPhone, and the at a glance table shows two more apps with no purchases: Snore Timeline and Snore Recorder - Sleep Monitor. Most other recorders charge to play back what they recorded. The app notes say which.

    How do I record myself sleeping on an iPhone?

    Plug the phone in on the nightstand, one to four feet from your head, and start the recorder before you lie down. The setup steps cover threshold, quality and placement. The identification steps cover what to do the morning after.

    Will a fan or white noise ruin the recording?

    In a trigger-clip app, it can. The review section has examples of hundreds of fan clips and a whole night of fan noise. Step 5 of the setup steps covers a fan in Snore Timeline.

    I recorded a sound I can’t explain. What is it likely to be?

    Check the rhythm. A sound that repeats once per breath, in a long run, is snoring or groaning. A single sound with no rhythm is the room, a pet, a partner or the house. The field guide lists seven sounds and when to see a doctor.

    Do these apps upload my bedroom audio?

    Read the privacy label in the at a glance table. It’s the developer’s own declaration. Voice Memos declares Audio Data for iCloud sync. SnoreLab, ShutEye, Leap’s Sleep Tracker and Snore Recorder - Sleep Monitor also declare Audio Data. Snore Timeline has no server.
