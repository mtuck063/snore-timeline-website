Apps That Record Noises While You Sleep (2026)

By Menelik Tucker · Updated 9 Oct 2026

Quick answer

An app that records noises while you sleep keeps the microphone open all night and either saves everything or saves a clip each time its classifier recognises a sound. If you don’t know what the noise is yet, pick a whole-night recorder, because a clip app keeps only the sounds it can name. I make Snore Timeline, which records the whole night on iPhone and Android, labels snoring, gasps, coughs, sleep talking, laughter and breathing pauses, marks anything it couldn’t name so you can play it, and charges nothing, with the audio kept on your phone. The advice draws on 2,656 recent App Store reviews of nine recorders.

Key takeaways

  • Clips lose sounds: most recorders save trigger clips, so a sound the classifier can’t label may never be saved; SnoreLab keeps samples unless you pay [13] [17].
  • Reviews: across 2,656 recent App Store reviews of nine recorders, the subscription wall is the top complaint and a fan the top false trigger.
  • Prevalence: snoring is habitual in 40 percent of adult men and 24 percent of women; 60 to 65 percent of people have talked in their sleep [1] [2].
  • The gasp: snoring, a silent pause, then a gasp is the AASM’s pattern for sleep apnea; a recording shows it to a doctor and diagnoses nothing [8].
  • Screen locked: an iPhone app records locked only if its developer enabled background audio [11]; two apps here say stay open on screen [19] [23].

    How to work out what a noise was

    This is what I do on my own nights. The wave brightens about a second before a label can appear, so a bright apricot swell is a sound the app counted but couldn’t name; those hold the surprises.

    1. Play the apricot swells first. On the closest zoom they mark the sounds without a name: a door, a car, a groan.
    2. Check the rhythm. Snoring and groaning ride the breath, one event per breath in a train; a thump or a bark has no rhythm. Zoom to the 1-second bars.
    3. Check the clock. It sits under the centre line; a 2:14 a.m. sound that matches the heating, the cat door or a bathroom trip has its answer.
    4. Read the frequency colour. Dark orange is low rumble, 50 to 250 Hz, the snoring band; bright orange is highs, the sibilant “s” of speech or a hiss. All highs is not a snore.
    5. Listen with the room in it. Playback starts about two seconds early, and when a blanket muffled a sound or two overlapped, your ears settle it; the app gets things wrong sometimes.
    6. Transcribe the words. If it’s speech, on-device transcription writes it out, audio never leaving the phone, on nights recorded at Standard or High Fidelity; Basic trims the speech range.

    Two nights beat one: a sound that recurs at the same point, or only on nights you tag (wine, a late meal, a cold), tells you more than one clip; the remedy guide covers tagging.

    Set up a night that catches everything

    1. Plug it in on the nightstand, never under a pillow. Recording uses 5 to 7 percent of battery an hour, and heat shortens battery life and pauses charging [12].
    2. Turn on Loud Sound Detection. It’s off by default. Leave the threshold on Automatic, just above whatever your room measures, or fix it 15 to 20 dB above the quiet reading.
    3. Pick the tier for the sound. Basic (8 MB an hour) keeps the snoring band, Standard (15 MB) keeps speech to 8,000 Hz, and only High Fidelity (25 MB) keeps breathing audible.
    4. Place it for the sound you suspect. Three to four feet for snoring, one to two feet for breathing and speech, microphone unobstructed.
    5. Quiet the fan if you can. A fan creates no false events in Snore Timeline, though it masks the quiet ones, so move the phone closer and raise the sensitivity.
    6. Cap the storage. A 2 GB limit holds one to two weeks at high quality and deletes the oldest nights for you; with History on, the episode data survives the audio.
    7. Tag the night. Alcohol, caffeine, a cold, a late meal: after two tagged and two untagged nights it ranks what changed.
    Medical note

    Snore Timeline analyzes audio on your phone. It is not a medical device and does not diagnose sleep apnea, bruxism, REM sleep behaviour disorder or any other condition. Its sleep stages are estimates. If a recording holds gasping, choking, long silences between breaths, shouting or violent movement, take it to a doctor.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is the best app to record noises while you sleep?

    If you don’t know the noise yet, start with one that keeps the whole night. The at a glance table shows which apps do, and which play it back without paying.

    Is there a free app that records you sleeping?

    Yes: Voice Memos comes with the iPhone, and the at a glance table shows two apps with no purchases. Most other recorders charge to play back what they recorded; the app notes say which.

    How do I record myself sleeping on an iPhone?

    Plug the phone in on the nightstand, one to four feet from your head, with the recorder open before you lie down. The setup steps cover threshold, quality and placement; the identification steps cover the morning after.

    Will a fan or white noise ruin the recording?

    In a trigger-clip app it can; the review table has the fan clips and the whole night of fan. Step 5 of the setup steps covers a fan in Snore Timeline.

    I recorded a sound I can’t explain. What is it likely to be?

    Check the rhythm: once per breath in a train is snoring or groaning, and a single sound with no rhythm is the room, a pet, a partner or the house. The field guide lists seven sounds and when to see a doctor.

    Do these apps upload my bedroom audio?

    Read the privacy label in the at a glance table; it’s the developer’s own declaration. Voice Memos declares Audio Data for iCloud sync, SnoreLab, ShutEye, Leap’s Sleep Tracker and Snore Recorder - Sleep Monitor declare Audio Data, and Snore Timeline has no server.
